# 0017: One scheduler per process, ranked by visible area

- **Status:** Accepted (T3.3). T4.2 added present groups, `Retarget`, and a fix for a redraw lost to a pause ([ADR 0020](0020-atlas-compositor.md)); T4.3 added group beats ([ADR 0021](0021-atlas-scope-and-content-rate.md)). T6.1 made a job decode before it acquires the sink's buffer and made removal, retargeting and fewer workers cancel a frame in flight instead of waiting for it ([ADR 0027](0027-cancellable-frames.md)), which changes the waits in decisions 7–9.
- **Date:** 2026-11-11

## Context

Until T3.3 each animation had its own clock: a native render thread per `SurfacePlayer` on Android, a serial queue and `DispatchSourceTimer` per `StickerTexture` on iOS. PLAN.md ("Scheduler") replaces them with one clock thread and N workers. Building it settled where it lives, how it reaches the platform buffers, what "late frames are dropped" means for codecs whose frames depend on each other, how `maxConcurrent` learns which stickers matter most, and how the threads behave on each platform.

## Decision

1. **The scheduler is in the core** (`src/sched/`), shared by both platforms. `wt::Timeline` holds one animation's timing rules and takes the time as an argument, so they are unit-tested with a fake clock. `wt::Scheduler` runs the threads.
2. **C ABI** (`WT_API_VERSION` 4): `wt_sched_create/destroy/set_options/get_stats` and `wt_player_create/destroy/set_active/set_priority/seek/redraw`. A player owns its `wt_anim` and draws through a **`wt_sink`**: `acquire` hands out a destination buffer, `release` shows it (or drops a failed frame), and `error` reports the first decode failure. Acquire may answer **`WT_BUSY`** (try next tick) or **`WT_NO_TARGET`** (wait for `wt_player_redraw`), two new non-error codes. Optional **thread hooks** run on every scheduler thread. Each glue creates one scheduler per process and never destroys it.
3. **The clock** runs at most `max_fps` rounds a second, and only when a player is due. With nothing playing it waits without a timeout, so a paused app costs no CPU. Requests (the first frame, a seek, a redraw) skip the round limit and go to a worker at once, playing or not.
4. **One frame in flight per player.** A round skips a player whose frame is still being drawn. Playback follows the content's own clock: when the next frame is due, a round also passes over every later frame that is already due. Those frames are decoded but not converted or shown (`wt::Anim::SkipFrame`), so VP8/VP9 references stay intact and a first pass with skipped frames still writes the `.wsc` cache. Read from the cache, a skip costs nothing. A frame more than `max(2 rounds, 100 ms)` late (suspension, a stalled worker) instead restarts the clock from the next frame, so the scheduler never races to catch up. Frames shorter than 10 ms count as 10 ms. A `max_fps` below the content rate therefore keeps the content's speed and shows fewer frames.
5. **`maxConcurrent`** is applied to active players that can move (more than one frame, not a non-looping one that ended). It ranks them by **priority**, then **recency**, the most recently activated first. The rest freeze on their frame and keep its remaining time for when they win a slot back.
6. **Priority is the on-screen area in physical pixels**, sent by Dart through a new Pigeon call `setPriority(animId, priority)`. `StickerVisibility` reports the visible fraction of each widget rounded up to quarters, so a scroll sends at most four updates per sticker. The registry adds up the areas of an animation's active leases and sends the sum only while the animation is active and only when it changes. `setActive` keeps its signature.
7. **Defaults** come from `wt_sched_options_init_default`: workers are half the CPU cores, clamped to 1–4; `max_fps` is 60; `max_concurrent` is 0 (no limit). The display refresh rate isn't read: stickers are at most 60 fps, and T3.4 lowers the cap per tier. T3.4 gets the options to the scheduler through the glue that owns it (a Pigeon call into `wt_sched_set_options`). Runtime changes apply at the next round, and fewer workers wait for their frame in flight.
8. **Android:** `SurfacePlayer` is now just the sink. `acquire` locks the window mutex and the `ANativeWindow` buffer, and `release` posts and unlocks, on the same worker. `SetWindow(nullptr)` therefore still waits for at most one write before `onSurfaceCleanup` returns. A missing window answers `WT_NO_TARGET` and a new one calls `wt_player_redraw`. The thread hooks attach every scheduler thread to the JVM once, under its own name (ADR 0011's reason).
9. **iOS:** `WTPlayer` (formerly `WTAnimation`) is the sink. Each frame goes into a fresh pool buffer, with the pool's allocation threshold of 4 answering `WT_BUSY`, and the newest frame is kept for `copyPixelBuffer`. The hooks give scheduler threads user-initiated QoS. `close` waits for a frame in flight, so the Swift glue runs it on a background queue after unregistering the texture. The first frame is now drawn by the scheduler right after creation, so `createAnimation` no longer fails when frame 0 doesn't decode; that error arrives on `streamErrors`, as it already did on Android.
10. **Verification:** host tests cover the timeline rules with a fake clock and the scheduler with fake sources and sinks that check its promises: one callback at a time per player, release on the acquiring thread, nothing after destroy. A stress test runs 500 animations churning through eight threads plus an options tuner, and runs under ASan and under a new `-DWT_TSAN=ON` build (a CI job). `example/integration_test/sched_perf_test.dart` measures raster time and CPU on a device.

## Consequences

- Frames of different stickers land in the same rounds, so Flutter rasterises once per round instead of once per sticker per frame.
- Every sticker shares 1–4 decode threads. One slow decode delays the others in its round, and a player that falls behind skips frames to keep its timing.
- `maxConcurrent` needs visible areas from Dart. Players driven without them, such as the raw Pigeon calls in the integration tests, rank by recency alone.
- Scheduler threads live as long as the process. They cost nothing while idle.
- The skipped frames of a first, uncached pass are still decoded. Below the content rate, `max_fps` saves conversion and buffer traffic before the cache exists, and nearly everything after.
